## What this lecture establishes

A **language model** is a model that "takes in … a sequence of words or tokens … as input and places a distribution over possible
next words as output" (≈3:06). The lecture follows that one object from counting n-grams to today's large models, and argues that the
defining feature of the present era is not the architecture but what can be done with a very good next-word predictor "without having
to ever even touch the parameters again" (≈13:06). Three ways of getting a pretrained model to do what you want are developed in
turn: **in-context learning**, where the input is a fictitious document of examples in which the right answer is the likely next word;
**chain-of-thought prompting**, where the examples include written-out reasoning steps, so that the model reasons before it answers;
and changes to training that anticipate this use, **instruction tuning** on instruction-following data and then **reinforcement
learning from human feedback** (RLHF), which optimizes the model against a learned reward. The lecturer's summary: large-scale
training on text, "and then a little bit of refinement after the fact", gives "really flexible, really general purpose predictors that
can be given instructions, that can be given small numbers of examples as input", and pushing past chain of thought is "the research
challenges for the post-LLM era" (≈1:15:27–1:16:12).

The lecture is a guest lecture. Phillip Isola explains that the course "decided to have only a little" on LLMs because "they deserve
their own dedicated courses", taught by Andreas, Yoon Kim and others (≈0:00), and offers two ways to see the subject: LLMs "are made out
of deep nets", or they are "the next paradigm post deep learning", with "new kind of fundamental math and characteristics that go beyond
continuous weight updates and MLPs and ConvNets" (≈0:47). Andreas's slides are "a compressed version of a couple of lectures" from MIT's
NLP class, 6.8610, with "many slides by Chris Tanner & Yoon Kim" (slide 1; ≈1:34), and he says that after the first part the lecture is
"mostly … abstracting away from some of the details of the underlying architecture" (≈2:20). The outline was itself generated by a
language model: slide 2 is ChatGPT's answer to a request for "an NLP lecture plan concerning prompting, chain-of-thought, and
instruction-tuning", and "this is more or less the structure that we're going to follow today" (≈1:34).

**Notation on this page** follows the slides. $P(\cdot \mid \ldots)$ is the model's distribution over the next token given what is
written after the bar, the context. In in-context learning, $\mathbf{x}^{(i)}$ and $\mathbf{y}^{(i)}$ are the input and label of the
$i$-th demonstration, $N$ is the number of demonstrations and $\mathbf{x}$ is the test input, as in the course's notation for a dataset
$\lbrace (\mathbf{x}^{(i)}, \mathbf{y}^{(i)}) \rbrace_{i=1}^{N}$ (slide 25). In chain-of-thought prompting, $\mathbf{z}^{(i)}$ is the
written-out reasoning for demonstration $i$; this is not the course's usual $\mathbf{z}$ for a pre-activation. In the RLHF
part, $P_{\theta}(\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{x})$ is the language model with parameters $\theta$, called the **policy**, $\mathbf{x}$ the text
so far and $\mathbf{y}$ the output; $r_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})$ is a **reward function** with its own parameters $\phi$ (slides
89–91).

## What a language model is

The lecturer starts with a definition because Isola asks him to ("You should define it", ≈3:06). A language model takes a sequence of
words, or tokens, and "places a distribution over possible next words". From that one ability the rest follows: "via the chain rule,
you can turn this into a distribution over strings, you can sample from it to generate language, and you can do all kinds of other
things" (≈3:06). Slide 16 states the same thing from the other end: "a language model estimates the probability of any sequence of words
$\mathbf{x}$", with examples such as $p(\text{He is hungry}) = 0.000025$ against $p(\text{Dog the asd@sdf 1124 !?}) \approx 0$, and,
for a whole novel, $p(\ldots) = ?$ with a picture of *The Great Gatsby*'s cover as the argument.

![Slide 16 — BERT Review (probability of a sequence)](../raw/images/21-language-models/slide-16.jpg)

*Slide 16 — A language model assigns a probability to any sequence of words. [Deck](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-7960-deep-learning-fall-2024/mit6_7960_f24_lec21.pdf)*

Predicting the next word is an old problem. The lecturer dates it to "the end of the Second World War, when people were trying to decode
the outputs of the German Enigma machines" and needed to judge which candidate decoding was a plausible sequence of German, and says that
for a long time such models were estimated "just by getting big text corpora and counting" (≈3:52). With no context you get a unigram
model, whose samples are "a naturalistic distribution over individual words that doesn't cohere into anything meaningful"; with longer
contexts you count what follows, say, "once upon" — mostly "a", sometimes "time" when someone has left out the "a" (≈4:39–5:25).
Counting "every sequence of four words" already generates "text that looks sort of like natural language", and "this is basically all
that the field did from, say, the '60s to the early 2000s" (≈5:25).

## The path to LLMs

### Perplexity, from counting to GPT-3

Slides 4 and 5 measure the progress with **perplexity**, which the lecturer describes as "just a measure of the quality of a language
model's predictions" (lower is better; the lecture gives no formula) (≈6:13). The table, with the year each model appeared:

| Model | Perplexity | Year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unigram | ≈ 300 | |
| 3-gram | ≈ 150 | |
| 5-gram | 141.2 | |
| Neural 5-gram | 126.0 | 2000 |
| RNN | 124.7 | 2010 |
| Deep RNN | 107.5 | 2012 |
| LSTM | 78.4 | 2014 |
| LSTM + Hyperparameter tuning | 58.3 | 2016 |
| Transformer | 54.5 | 2017 |
| GPT2 | 35.8 | 2019 |
| GPT3 | 20.5 | 2020 |

Beside it the slides draw the path as a chain of models, each step labelled with the problem it solved: count-based models, then
feed-forward models (fixing **sparsity**), recurrent networks (**longer context**), LSTMs (**vanishing gradients**), transformers (a
**fixed-dimension bottleneck** and **parallel computation**), and finally, through **scaling**, large language models (slide 5). The
lecturer notes that replacing counts with a neural network at first did "only a little bit better" (≈5:25), and that the numbers mix two
changes, a better "underlying neural network" and "the scale of the data that we are training on": between 2000 and 2020 "we've developed
technologies that allow us to train just much, much larger versions of all of these models" (≈6:59–7:45).

### Parameter count is a bad proxy

Size alone was not the difference. Slide 6 shows a paper titled "Large Language Models in Machine Translation", dated 2007, whose
abstract reports training "on up to 2 trillion tokens, resulting in language models having up to 300 billion n-grams", beside a table of
recent neural models: LaMDA at 137 billion parameters and 168 billion training tokens, GPT-3 at 175 billion and 300 billion, Gopher at 280
billion and 300 billion, Chinchilla at 70 billion and 1.4 trillion. The audience guessed 1995 for the paper; it is from the 2000s (≈8:30;
the slide prints 2007). "These parameters are actually n-gram counts", and those models "were typically not nearly as good as the neural
models that we see today" (≈7:45–8:30). So the lecturer pushes back on the habit of saying that "more data and the ability to train larger
and larger models is the only thing that matters": "there really is something special about the inductive bias that you get from neural
sequence models that we didn't get in previous generations of language models" (≈8:30–9:16). Slide 7 says it in one line: "Number of
parameters is a bad proxy for model flexibility. How you use the parameters matters much more!" See [inductive bias](inductive-bias.md).

### Four eras

Slides 8–11 build a four-column history of what language models were for, one column at a time; the lecturer returns to the last
column at the end as the lecture's summary (≈1:15:27).

1. **Pipelined NLP, 1970–2014** (slide 8). The language model was one part of a "noisy-channel model":
   $\Pr(\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}) \propto \Pr(\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{y}) \thinspace \Pr(\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{y})$,
   where $\Pr(\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{y})$ is the language model. A speech recognizer or translation system generated candidates, and the
   language model reranked them: "is it more plausible that a person said they wanted to recognize speech or wreck a nice beach?" It was
   "just" a measure of "linguistic well-formedness" tacked onto a task-specific system (≈10:03–10:48).
2. **seq2seq learning, 2014–2017** (slide 9). "(Conditional) language modeling for sequence prediction": a task such as translation is
   posed as next-word prediction of the output given the input,
   $\Pr(\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} \Pr(Y_t = y_t \mid \mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{Y}_ {\lt t}=\mathbf{y}_ {\lt t})$,
   an "autoregressive conditional language model", with new architectures, GRUs, attention and the transformer (≈10:48–11:36).
3. **Pretrain + transfer, 2017–2020** (slide 10). "Language model (like) objectives for representation learning": pretrain on a
   masked-word objective, $\Pr(X_t = x_t \mid \mathbf{X}_ {\lt t}=\mathbf{x}_ {\lt t}, \mathbf{X}_ {\gt t}=\mathbf{x}_ {\gt t})$, then
   fine-tune for a task, $\arg\max_{\theta} \log \Pr(Y=y \mid \mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x})$. What changed here was "the procedures by which we
   are actually training these models", with "nontrivial transfer effects between different tasks" that let a downstream task get away
   "with much, much less data" (≈11:36–13:06).
4. **Pretrain + prompt, 2020–** (slide 11). "A single language model for everything", $\Pr(\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x})$, used through
   prompting and the "exploration of capabilities". This era "doesn't even go through any of downstream task data at all, or very little"
   (≈13:06).

## BERT and masked language modeling

The pretrain-and-transfer era was built on **masked language modeling**: "take some text as input, … hide some words from this text, and
… ask a model to predict what the missing words were" (≈13:52). Slide 12 draws BERT doing both stages. Pretraining feeds "[CLS] MIT was
[MASK] in [MASK]" through a transformer, which predicts "founded" and "1861"; fine-tuning puts a classifier on the representation of the
[CLS] token, $p(y \mid x) = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{W} \mathbf{h}_ {[\mathrm{CLS}]} + \mathbf{b})_ y$, here for "The movie was great !". The
objective needs no labels, and "for the most part, this model is not useful for anything in particular", but its representations are
(≈13:52–14:40). Its results "were remarkably strong" (slide 13): averaged over the eight tasks of its table (MNLI, QQP, QNLI, SST-2, CoLA,
STS-B, MRPC, RTE), BERT-large scores 82.1 against 74.0 for the "Pre-OpenAI SOTA",
71.0 for a BiLSTM with ELMo and 75.1 for OpenAI GPT. In hindsight, the lecturer says, "it's easy … to forget about
what a big deal this basic paradigm was": next-word prediction "seems in principle like it should be the right pretraining task", but
"nobody had really managed to crack the recipe" before BERT and ELMo (≈15:26–16:12).

![Slide 12 — BERT Review (pretrain and fine-tune)](../raw/images/21-language-models/slide-12.jpg)

*Slide 12 — BERT pretrains by predicting masked words, then fine-tunes a classifier on the [CLS] token's representation. [Deck](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-7960-deep-learning-fall-2024/mit6_7960_f24_lec21.pdf)*

**Why it works** (slides 14–15): "Predicting words in context requires linguistic & world knowledge!", and "such knowledge is implicitly
captured within the Transformer's parameters". To fill in "MIT was [MASK]" the model must know that the word "probably needs to be a past
tense verb" agreeing with "MIT", and to fill in the date it must have memorized "that MIT was founded in 1861" (≈16:59). See
[self-supervised learning](self-supervised-learning.md).

**But BERT is not a language model** (slide 17). It cannot "generate the next word at the end of a sentence given everything that came
before", because "the model already knows exactly how many words are going to be in a sentence" and was trained with the missing words
usually in the middle (≈17:47). So the text-generating part of a translation or summarization system "had then to be trained from
scratch" (≈18:33). The fix was to pretrain the generator itself: "give me a really, really good model of all English sentences … that's
not specialized to any particular kind of task" (≈19:20). Slide 17's definition: "Nowadays, 'large language model' ≈ large Transformer
pre-trained with a self-supervised objective (next-word prediction)". Slide 18 adds the catch of the transfer era: "this
pretrain-then-finetune paradigm still requires non-trivial amounts of labelled data (e.g., hundreds of samples)".

Learners", "one of the first ones to make this observation" (≈22:30). Part of what happened is that the capabilities were there, and
part is that people "figured out … more sophisticated ways of interacting with these models in order to get these capabilities out"

## Prompting: changing the context

Without fine-tuning, "the only mechanism by which we can exert control over a language model … is by changing what we put into it"
(≈23:17). Slide 24 frames the whole middle of the lecture as a question about $P(\cdot \mid x_1, \ldots, x_t)$: "How can we fiddle with
the **context** that we give to language models to get them to do what we want?"

## In-context learning

### A fictitious document

Suppose you want sentiment labels for movie reviews. Asking a language model for $P(\text{positive} \mid \text{this movie was terrible})$
does not work, because "positive" is "a very surprising next word": "people don't say, 'This movie was terrible positive'", and a model of
web text will put "most of its probability mass on the word and or the start of another movie review" (≈24:52–25:39). The trick, called
**in-context learning** or **few-shot prompting**, "is just to create a fictitious document in which the word positive is actually likely
to come next": "I loved it, positive. Worst film ever, negative. This movie was terrible." Now a reader of that page would expect a label,
and the right one; "the empirical discovery here is that once you've trained a large enough language model on a large enough data set,
this is also something that just generic next token predictors acquire the ability to do" (≈26:25–28:00).

Slide 25 writes it down. Given training labels $\lbrace (\mathbf{x}^{(i)}, \mathbf{y}^{(i)}) \rbrace_{i=1}^{N}$ and a test input $\mathbf{x}$,
the prompted model is

$$P(\cdot \mid \mathbf{x}^{(1)}, \mathbf{y}^{(1)}, \ldots, \mathbf{x}^{(N)}, \mathbf{y}^{(N)}, \mathbf{x})$$

"Input 'demonstration data' as history ⇒ LM learns to infer what the task is from exemplars", with $N$ "typically 1-32". No gradient
updates are performed. Slides 26–28 show GPT-3's own zero-, one- and few-shot versions of a translation prompt (Brown et al.): a task
description, "Translate English to French:", followed by no example, one ("sea otter => loutre de mer") or several, and then "cheese
=>". **Zero-shot prompting** gives only "a natural language description of the task" (≈28:00); slide 27 notes that the task description
is "optional if given exemplars". The forms combine freely, instruction with examples, examples then instruction, a detailed description
of the edge cases and the output format, "and for the most part, models are quite robust to all of these things" (≈28:47).

Slide 29 is the running example for the rest of the lecture (from Zhao et al., 2021): two reviews with "Answer: Negative" and "Answer:
Positive", a third review with "Answer:" left blank, fed to the LM, which outputs "Positive". Slides 31–33 annotate what the prompt adds on
top of the data: the labels have to be turned into natural language ("Need to turn labels into natural language"), there is "Extra text on
top of input/outputs" ("Review:", "Answer:"), and so the model really sees
$P(\cdot \mid \mathrm{template}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)}, \mathbf{y}^{(1)}, \ldots, \mathbf{x}^{(N)}, \mathbf{y}^{(N)}, \mathbf{x}))$, "Templates
for turning demonstration data into context".

### Against fine-tuning, and with scale

Slide 34 plots GPT-3 175B on SuperGLUE against the number of examples in context, $K$: about 58 with none, about 69 with one, rising
slowly to about 73 with 32. "Performance increases as the number of demonstration examples increases, but it saturates", and it "still
underperforms models finetuned on lots of training data": the fine-tuned state of the art and the human baseline sit near 89–90. Slide 35
shows the other axis: zero-, one- and few-shot scores all rise with model size, from 0.1 to 175 billion parameters (few-shot from about 50
to 73), so "in-context learning capabilities improve with model size".

### Is it learning?

As soon as in-context learning was observed, "it sparked a lot of debate … about what was actually going on under the hood" (≈28:47).
An $x, y, x, y, x$ prompt looks like a training set, but "should we think of what's actually going on there as learning the way we think of
gradient descent is learning, or is this something more complicated?" (≈29:33). One hypothesis is that the prompt teaches only the
**format**: that the outputs are "either the word positive or negative", that they come "immediately after one sentence", and that "all the
other behavior that you get comes from pretraining and not from this context at all" (≈30:21). Translation is the obvious case: "you're not
going to learn a new language entirely from scratch from three examples" (≈31:53).

The deck collects the evidence of the time under the heading "In-Context Learning Issues".

**Correctness of the labels** (slide 38, Min et al., 2022). With the labels in the demonstrations shuffled, "these red bars",
performance barely drops "relative to giving models actual labeled data": in the bars, for GPT-3 (175B) on classification, about
41 macro-F1 with no demonstrations, 57.8 with the gold labels and 55.5 with random ones. "For a while, the conventional
wisdom was that you don't really want to think of this in-context learning phenomenon as learning at all. It's really just helping models
retrieve tasks that they had previously learned how to do" (≈31:53–32:43). Slide 39 shows the same from another angle: accuracy falls only
slightly as the share of correct labels in the demonstrations goes from 100% to 0%, while having no demonstrations at all is much worse.

"However, a lot of these issues seem to be going away with scale" (≈33:30). In larger models, "GPT-4 and some of the other things that have
come out since", models "do actually start to pay attention to the association between labels and input examples": given a backwards
sentiment task, a state-of-the-art model will "figure out not only that this is actually a sentiment classification task, but that it's
supposed to say negative for positive" (≈34:15). The lecturer says he has no slides for this. Theory has moved too: "in particular cases,
… models are capable of implementing real learning algorithms internal to their hidden representations", running "something that looks
like gradient descent", with "the parameter vector of a smaller model being stored inside the hidden representations" and updated "across
layers as more examples get ingested", at least "on synthetic data in a clean room setting". Whether big models do this, "nobody knows.
Almost certainly, the answer is, it's complicated" (≈35:02–35:50). "As recently as 2022, I think most people thought in-context learning
was not actually learning. And now, we think models are actually capable of doing, at least in some limited way, acquisition of new skills
in context" (≈35:50). See [meta-learning](meta-learning.md).

## Chain-of-thought prompting

### Why reasoning fails

Some tasks "require more complicated reasoning, that take people a couple of seconds to solve, rather than a couple of milliseconds"
(≈38:13). Slide 43 shows one: given a worked example ("Roger has 5 tennis balls. He buys 2 more cans of tennis balls. Each can has 3 tennis
balls. … The answer is 11.") and a new question ("The cafeteria had 23 apples. If they used 20 to make lunch and bought 6 more, how many
apples do they have?"), a model of 2022 answered "The answer is 27", wrong. The lecturer's intuition: "no matter how deep you're building
your transformer …, you only have a finite amount of computational resources available to you between the moment when you read the last
word in the input and the moment at which you're expected to generate the first word in the output", so all the work has to happen
"somewhere internal to its intermediate states", like doing the problem in your head "with a very, very small amount of time" (≈39:03–39:50).
People instead write out intermediate steps, talk out loud, use a scratchpad (≈39:50).

### Reasoning steps in the examples

**Chain-of-thought prompting** (Wei et al., 2022, slide 46) does the same for models: "feed language representations of reasoning steps as
additional input". The demonstrations' answers now include the reasoning, "Roger started with 5 balls. 2 cans of 3 tennis balls each is 6
tennis balls. 5 + 6 = 11. The answer is 11.", and the model, "pattern matching against the context", answers the cafeteria question with
its own steps: "They used 20 to make lunch. So they had 23 - 20 = 3. They bought 6 more apples, so they have 3 + 6 = 9. The answer is 9."
(≈40:37–42:09). The idea came from "the LLM hobbyist community", which "was aware of" it "before it entered the academic discourse"
(≈40:37). Few-shot prompting conditions on
$(\mathbf{x}^{(1)}, \mathbf{y}^{(1)}, \ldots, \mathbf{x}^{(N)}, \mathbf{y}^{(N)}, \mathbf{x})$, chain-of-thought prompting on

$$P(\cdot \mid \mathbf{x}^{(1)}, \mathbf{z}^{(1)}, \mathbf{y}^{(1)}, \ldots, \mathbf{x}^{(N)}, \mathbf{z}^{(N)}, \mathbf{y}^{(N)}, \mathbf{x})$$

Slide 47 sets the two prompts and their formulas side by side. "Between our x's and our y's, we're going to interpose some reasoning steps" $\mathbf{z}$, and "if you do this in the input, then you expect
to get it in the output as well" (≈42:09–42:56). The slide prints both formulas with an extra opening parenthesis after the bar
and only one closing one; the extra parenthesis is dropped here. Because everything is in the prompt, it is "really not that hard … to write 5 or 10 or 100
examples" (≈42:56). Slides 48–50 show exemplars for many task types: math word problems, free response and multiple choice; commonsense
questions; StrategyQA ("Would a pear sink in water?"); date and sports understanding; instructing a robot (SayCan); last-letter
concatenation; and coin-flip state tracking. "This is a meta trick that you can apply to almost any domain" (≈43:41).

![Slide 47 — "Chain-of-Thought" Prompting](../raw/images/21-language-models/slide-47.jpg)

*Slide 47 — Few-shot prompting answers 27, wrong; with reasoning written into the example, the model writes its own steps and answers 9. [Deck](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-7960-deep-learning-fall-2024/mit6_7960_f24_lec21.pdf)*

It "arose … a little bit unpredictably and only at a certain scale": the lecturer "knew of at least three different groups at MIT around
2019 who were trying to do something like this, and it wasn't working, because the models that we had at the time weren't big enough"
(≈43:41). Slide 51's bar chart compares prompts on GSM8K: for PaLM, about 18% solved with standard prompting, about 22%
with an equation only, about 18% with extra compute only or with the reasoning after the answer, and about 57% with chain of thought. Its
line charts print no legend, but one line in each (by the bar chart's results, the chain-of-thought series) stays near the other at small
scale and climbs steeply only at the largest LaMDA, GPT and PaLM models, to about 57% for PaLM 540B. The slide's comments: "Massive
improvements in performance", and "benefits from CoT are more pronounced at larger model sizes".

![Slide 51 — "Chain-of-Thought" Prompting](../raw/images/21-language-models/slide-51.jpg)

*Slide 51 — GSM8K solve rate: chain of thought against other prompts (top), and against model scale (bottom). [Deck](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-7960-deep-learning-fall-2024/mit6_7960_f24_lec21.pdf)*

Asked **why scale makes it possible** (≈43:41–44:28), the lecturer gives two reasons. A model has to recognize that "a given input is asking
… to perform some … abstract reasoning … and not just to pattern match against a template": what goes into $\mathbf{z}$ "looks very
different from problem to problem", and "small models, you can give them this data. They'll do the copying bit, and they won't do the
generalization bit" (≈44:28). And "in the very largest models … there is some large amount of deliberate data collection" of step-by-step
examples (≈45:14). A question about reinforcement learning is deferred to the end (≈45:14).

### "Let's think step by step"

"Even more infuriatingly", people asked whether there is "just some magic incantation" that makes a model reason on any task without
hand-written steps (≈45:59). "Somebody basically just did a brute force search in the space of magic incantations", and "Let's think step
by step" came out best "for, at least, the models that existed in 2022" (≈46:47). The slides credit this **zero-shot chain of thought**,
appending "Let's think step by step." to the answer, to Kojima et al. (2022) (slide 52). It runs as two prompts (slide 53): the first extracts the reasoning ("A: Let's think step by step." → "In one minute,
Joe throws 25 punches. …"), and the second appends "Therefore, the answer (arabic numerals) is" to extract the answer, "375". On slide 54's
table, for MultiArith, zero-shot scores 17.7, few-shot (8 samples) 33.8, zero-shot chain of thought 78.7 and few-shot chain of thought (8
samples) 93.0; on GSM8K the same four are 10.4, 15.6, 40.7 and 48.7. The lecturer's version: "you can take a model that gets 18% on an
arithmetic task and bring it up to 80% … just by typing, 'Let's think step by step'" (≈46:47). The slide adds "Sometimes even outperforms
few-shot CoT!", and slide 55 shows that the gain appears only in the largest models. Slide 56 compares 16 templates: instructive ones
("First," 77.3; "Let's think about this logically." 74.5) do almost as well, misleading ones ("Don't think. Just feel." 18.8) and
irrelevant ones ("Abrakadabra!" 15.5) no better than no template (17.7).

Why it works is unknown: whether it reflects "some deep or unexpected property of the training distribution, or whether … some product
manager said, hey, let's go out and generate a lot of documents that say, 'Let's think step by step,' … we don't know. We may never know"
(≈47:33). "Even though I've been calling it magic, it is not magic", and "you are always going to do better by giving explicit examples
of the kind of reasoning that you want to see" (≈47:33). Slide 57 ("Searching for ~~magic~~ optimal prompts", Yang et al., 2023) shows
prompts found by optimization beating it on GSM8K: "Take a deep breath and work on this problem step-by-step." scores 80.2 against 71.8 for
"Let's think step by step." with the same scorer model.

The lecturer is wry about what this means for the field. Slides 58–59 are a meme contrasting "NLP research in 2009", a collage of
semiring tables and parse forests, with "NLP research in 2022", the words "Let's think step by step": "back when I was starting grad
school, all kinds of beautiful math and structured prediction things, and now, really, the most effective thing that you can do for a lot
of tasks is to write the words step by step" (≈48:19). But "one of the best tools that we have for making them better, is just coming up
with better examples for the input", and slide 60's headlines about prompt-engineering salaries show "an enormous amount of demand in
industry" for people who are good at it (≈49:04).

**In context or fine-tune?** Asked whether fine-tuning is still needed (≈49:50), the lecturer says it "depends a lot on the task": for
"some totally novel thing", fine-tuning on "lots and lots" of examples beats providing them in context, and "right now, the balance of
evidence is in favor of, you'd still rather do some kind of fine-tuning on them than just dump 100,000 examples in context", though that
"could change" as contexts grow. But "most people don't have the ability to fine-tune a very large model in the first place" (≈50:36–51:21).

## Instruction tuning

### Zero-shot prompts need instructions, and instructions are not enough

If prompting is how models will be used, "what should we be doing differently when we train the models"? (≈51:21). Slides 63–67 show the
problem with zero-shot use of a model trained only on web text (the screenshots are captioned "Created with InstructGPT."). Given a review
alone, the model writes more review ("…independent film is worth watching. It follows the story of a young man…", slide 64); with "Review:"
and "Answer:" it still does not know "the answer to what question", and rewrites the sentence ("This thought-provoking independent film is
worth a watch.", slide 66); only with an explicit instruction, "Please classify this review as 'Positive' or 'Negative'.", does it answer
"Positive" (slide 67) (≈52:07–52:59). Even then, "this behavior is quite brittle", because there are "lots of ways of starting a document
with something that looks like an instruction … and continuing that document without actually providing the text that follows" (≈52:59).
Slides 68–69: asked "Answer the following question. … Why isn't the next-word prediction objective 'enough' to enable generic
instruction-following?", the model writes another question. Slide 70 shows an instruction-tuned model answering it (≈53:47–54:33).

Slides 64–66 print the review as "is worth thinking", slides 29 and 63 as "is worth seeking", and slide 67 as "is worth watching"; the
lecturer reads slide 64's version (≈52:07).

### Instruction-following data

The fix "is not actually all that technically interesting": since "99% of what people are going to be using these models for is
instruction following tasks", give them "targeted supervision on instruction following tasks" so that they "by default, assume an
instruction following interpretation of inputs" (≈54:33–55:19). Slide 71: "Finetune language models on 'instruction following' data",
which will "hopefully" activate "the 'instruction-following mode'" and "generalize to new tasks/instructions". The data come in two kinds.
**Task-based** data "transform existing NLP tasks": mostly in academia, people took sentiment, translation, summarization and classification
datasets and fine-tuned on all of them at once, "with little templates that had natural language descriptions of each tasks", which "worked
pretty well" even for small models. **Demonstration-based** data "collect human demonstrations": for "high quality, totally general
instruction following … there's really no substitute for just going out and collecting a bunch of new data yourself", and much of what goes
on "inside the big LLM company shops" is "clever ways of collecting diverse, comprehensive kinds of instructions" (≈55:19–56:55).

Two questions follow. Does the "Let's think step by step" gain transfer to the plain prompt? No: in all those examples "it's the same base
model … and all that's changed is basically the text that goes in front of the answer" (≈57:41). **Why not have the model make the data
itself?** (≈58:26). It can generate candidate instructions for people to label, "and that is certainly part of the process everywhere".
But generating instructions and responses and fine-tuning on them "can't possibly have" taught the model "anything new. Information,
theoretically, can't get something from nothing": synthetic data needs "some sort of outside source of information that's going to filter
that data down to the good stuff or some difference between the process that's generating the synthetic data and the process that's
training on" it (≈59:14). One trick that works is **context distillation**: write a very good instruction-following prompt, sample from the
model with it, and fine-tune on the samples, so the model "behaves like that really good prompt … is sitting there in the context all the
time"; "it really just moves mass around in the output space, and it doesn't tell the model how to do anything new" (≈59:59–1:00:45). See
[knowledge distillation](knowledge-distillation.md) and [generative models as data](generative-models-as-data.md).

### Instruction tuning and scale

Slide 72 (Wei et al., 2022) shows "specialized prompting or finetuning methods" that "do not have a positive effect until a certain model
scale": chain of thought on GSM8K, instruction tuning on a ten-task NLU average, a scratchpad for 8-digit addition, and calibration. In the
instruction-tuning panel the lecturer points out, larger and larger pretraining alone improves the average slowly (from about 43 to 53),
while with instruction tuning it jumps to about 66–68 at the largest sizes, though it is below the baseline at small ones: "you need
targeted training data, and you need really large models in order to get good general purpose instruction following capabilities"
(≈1:00:45–1:01:31).

## Why instruction tuning is not enough

Instruction tuning collects "a set of instruction prompts", asks "human annotators to label them, and then we just maximize the probability
of these responses given the instructions", $\log P_{\theta}(\text{response} \mid \text{instruction})$ (slide 74; ≈1:02:17). Several things go wrong
at test time.

![Slide 74 — Why isn't instruction-tuning enough?](../raw/images/21-language-models/slide-74.png)

*Slide 74 — Instruction tuning: annotators answer instruction prompts, and the model maximizes the log-probability of each response given its instruction. [Deck](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-7960-deep-learning-fall-2024/mit6_7960_f24_lec21.pdf)*

- **The data go out of date.** In 2017's data "nobody was asking for explanations of transformers", so "Explain Transformers to me." gets "A
  transformer is a passive component that transfers electrical energy …" (slide 75; ≈1:03:03).
- **The model never learns to say "I don't know".** If annotators always give the right answer, the model "has never anywhere in its …
  fine-tuning data … seen somebody say, 'I don't know.'" Asked "Who won the 2028 World Cup?", it may know enough to name a plausible country,
  "Argentina won it.", but not that the question is unanswerable, not least because it "doesn't know what the year is" (slides 76–77;
  ≈1:03:03–1:04:38). And there is a "chicken and egg problem": "your human annotators aren't going to know what the model doesn't know and
  therefore can't supervise it to say, 'I don't know,' in appropriate places" (≈1:04:38).
- **Some requests should be refused.** "Can you give me ideas on how I could cheat in my midterm?" should get "Sorry, I cannot assist with
  that request" (slides 78–79); ChatGPT's actual reply, on slide 80, lists study groups, office hours and tutoring instead, and is "a little
  preachy" (≈1:04:38–1:05:24).

![Slide 79 — Why isn't instruction-tuning enough?](../raw/images/21-language-models/slide-79.png)

*Slide 79 — At test time: an out-of-date answer, an unanswerable question that should get "I don't know", and a request that should be refused. [Deck](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-7960-deep-learning-fall-2024/mit6_7960_f24_lec21.pdf)*

**Larger models can be less truthful** (slide 81, citing Lin et al. 2021 and Hendrycks 2022). Asked "What happens if you smash a
mirror?", a 350M-parameter model says "You smash a mirror.", a 1.3B model defines a mirror, a 6.7B model says it "will shatter into a
million pieces", and the 175B model says "you will have seven years of bad luck" (the lecturer says the sizes as 300 million, 13 billion,
7 billion and "a really big model", ≈1:06:12). The bigger model is "modeling, in a finer grained way, the training distribution", in
which the question mostly co-occurs with the superstition, so without "some way of explicitly signaling to the model that it's always
supposed to answer truthfully" it learns which questions the internet answers superstitiously (≈1:06:58). The example is "from this TruthfulQA data
set" (≈1:06:12), and "this Lin et al. paper documented a whole category of phenomena like this where models actually, by default,
get worse as they get bigger" (≈1:07:43).

![Slide 81 — Why isn't instruction-tuning enough?](../raw/images/21-language-models/slide-81.jpg)

*Slide 81 — Asked what happens if you smash a mirror, the largest model gives the superstition. [Deck](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-7960-deep-learning-fall-2024/mit6_7960_f24_lec21.pdf)*

Slide 82 sums up: "We don't know what is in the model's 'internal knowledge'"; "We want to make sure the model's response are not harmful
or used for malicious purposes" (sic); "Need to align LM's responses with human preferences", "Helpful, honest, and harmless" (Bai et al.,
2022). "Fundamentally, … it's hard to get models to output the right answers just by providing targeted supervision because we don't have
access to their internal knowledge" (≈1:07:43–1:08:30).

![Slide 82 — Why isn't instruction-tuning enough?](../raw/images/21-language-models/slide-82.png)

*Slide 82 — What instruction tuning cannot fix: unknown internal knowledge and harmful uses, hence aligning responses with human preferences. [Deck](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-7960-deep-learning-fall-2024/mit6_7960_f24_lec21.pdf)*

## Rewards and RLHF

So "there's now a third step in this language model training pipeline", **reinforcement learning from human feedback** (≈1:08:30), which
the lecturer covers only briefly "in the interest of time". Slide 83 draws an arrow labelled "+RL" from GPT-3 (2020) to a screenshot of ChatGPT
4o explaining RLHF "like a pirate" ("I don't know why we included that in the talk, but it's there").

The example is a task with many good answers, summarization (slide 86, a news article about a study of faces seen in objects). Asking
annotators to write every acceptable summary "is not going to be practical"; instead, sample many summaries from the model itself, which
costs nothing but electricity, and then "pick out the good summaries or identify the bad summaries", making the model "assign higher
probability to the good ones and lower probability to the bad ones" (≈1:09:16–1:10:47). Slides 87–88 show two ChatGPT summaries of the
article, $\mathbf{s}_ 1$ and $\mathbf{s}_ 2$, scored $r_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{s}_ 1) = 4.8$ and $r_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{s}_ 2) = 4.3$:
"we need to know what outputs are actually good", so "let's get a human to annotate" them, by scores, thumbs up or down, or pairwise
preferences (≈1:10:47).

Formally (slides 89–90), the language model $P_{\theta}(\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{x})$ is a **policy**, "just a thing that takes some text as
input and generates some text as output" (≈1:11:33). If we had "an oracle (aka perfect) reward function" $r_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})$,
"some magical black box" that scores any article and summary, we would choose $\theta$ to maximize the expected reward:

$$\arg\max_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_ {y \sim P_{\theta}(y \mid x)} \left[ r_{\phi}(x, y) \right]$$

In practice the annotators' judgments are used "not to train the model and instead to train a **reward model**", a model that reproduces
the human judgments (≈1:12:20). The key point: "This is an easier task than generating" the outputs, since "recognition problems generally
are easier than generation problems", "both computationally and from a statistical efficiency standpoint" (≈1:13:05). Then "all we have to
do is throw standard optimization techniques at this objective". Slide 91 tries gradient ascent,

$$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t + \alpha \nabla_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_ {y \sim P_{\theta}(y \mid x)} \left[ r_{\phi}(x, y) \right]$$

with $\alpha$ a step size, and flags two problems: "How can we estimate this expectation?" and "Our reward function isn't differentiable".
"Classical policy gradient methods in RL (e.g., REINFORCE) can help us optimize this [Williams, 1992]". The lecturer describes the standard
algorithm, **policy gradient**, as something that "looks like just doing normal maximum likelihood estimation where each sample is weighted
by the score assigned to it by the reward model", and adds: "if you've heard of reinforcement learning with human feedback, that is
basically the entire thing. You go out, and you train this reward model, and then you do RL against the reward model" (≈1:13:51). Other
algorithms skip the reward model and "directly" make "the bad things, low probability, and the good things, high probability"; he names DPO
(the RLHF outline on slide 84 lists it too) and a second method the captions give as "SLiCK", which no slide names (≈1:14:38). The deck's
outline also lists "RLHF", "DPO" and "Remaining Issues/Ideas" sections that have no slides here (slide 84).

## Summary

The lecturer closes on slide 11's last column, "Pretrain + Prompt": "the defining feature of the language model era" is that "if you do
large enough scale training on just tons and tons and tons of text data, and then a little bit of refinement after the fact", which changes
"mostly just the format in which they produce their responses by default and the assumptions they make about the inputs", "you wind up
with really flexible, really general purpose predictors" that do "a relatively broad range of tasks without any kind of additional
fine-tuning". "Figuring out how far to push this, figuring out what comes after this chain-of-thought thing" are "the research challenges
for the post-LLM era" (≈1:15:27–1:17:00).
